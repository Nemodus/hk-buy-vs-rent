Buy or Rent / Hong Kong

A tool to help you decide whether buying a home or renting is the better choice in Hong Kong.

What this compares

Two ways to live in the same Hong Kong flat. In one you take a mortgage and buy it. In the other you rent it and put the same money into the markets instead.

The comparison is kept fair by forcing the cash flows to match: every dollar the owner spends in a month — mortgage, rates, management fee, insurance, repairs — the renter spends on rent and invests whatever is left over.

  1. 1Set the assumptions Price, deposit, mortgage terms, rent, running costs, tax. All in the Assumptions panel, and shared by every scenario.
  2. 2Write your scenarios On the Summary tab give each one a property growth rate, a rent growth rate, a portfolio return — and how likely you think it is.
  3. 3Analyse the results Summary for the probability-weighted result; a scenario tab for its position, cash flows, rates, sensitivity and risk.

Buy and rent, probability-weighted

Each line is the weighted average across every scenario at each year. The table above holds the scenarios themselves; the note below the next chart gives the range they span.

The two assets, side by side

Weighted flat value against the weighted portfolio, with the mortgage underneath both. The loan is the one line that is the same in every scenario, so it is drawn once rather than averaged.

Running gross rental yield

The weighted average of each scenario’s yield. The note underneath gives the range across your scenarios and names any that leave the band — a sign that whichever growth rate is driving it is doing something the market has not sustained.

What Hong Kong housing actually did

Official Rating and Valuation Department series, quarterly back to 1979. Use it to check whether the growth rates you have typed into your scenarios have any precedent.

Price and rent, both rebased to 100

On a log scale, so the same vertical distance is the same percentage move wherever it falls. The gap between the two lines is the whole argument: when price runs ahead of rent, yields compress and buying gets harder to justify on cash flow alone.

Calendar-year price change

Year-end to year-end. The point of this one is the size of the bars, not their average.

Gross rental yield by class

Rent over price, before any of the owner’s costs — the same measure the yield chart on a scenario tab reports. Your class is drawn solid, the others faintly for comparison.

What a long hold actually returned

Annualised price growth over every window of this length that has completed. This is the number to compare your scenarios against.

Net position through time

What each scenario is worth if you liquidated at that point — the flat sold net of costs and the mortgage repaid, against the portfolio plus the rental deposit.

The two assets, side by side

The flat is worth several times the portfolio for most of the term. The shaded band is the part of it you actually own — everything above the mortgage line.

Running gross rental yield

Rent paid over the year against what the flat is worth, gross — before any of the owner’s costs. A sanity check on whether the rent and price assumptions stay plausible together for thirty years.

Where the money goes each year

Both columns are the same height by construction: every dollar the owner spends in a year is a dollar the renter either pays in rent or puts into the market.

HIBOR, and what it does to your rate

A quarter century of Hong Kong rates on the left, your modelled path on the right. Your rate traces whichever of the two legs sits lower. Toggle series on and off to keep it readable.

Solid to the left of the divider is what happened; dashed and shaded to the right is the model. Historical rate levels are indicative annual averages, not official data.

What has to be true for buying to win
